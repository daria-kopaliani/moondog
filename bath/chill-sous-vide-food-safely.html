How to chill sous vide food safely

Pasteurizing kills active bacteria — but not spores. The danger is in how you cool.

Food-safety topic.

If you cook sous vide and then chill to store and reheat later, cooling speed is a real safety control, not a detail. The numbers below summarize Douglas Baldwin's guide and the FDA Food Code; they educate, they don't certify your specific batch safe.

Holding food at a precise temperature in a water bath pasteurizes it — it reduces active bacteria to a safe level. What pasteurization does not do is destroy bacterial spores. Clostridium perfringens and C. botulinum survive cooking as spores, and if the food then sits too long in the warm range while it cools, those spores germinate and multiply. The food that came out of the bath perfectly cooked becomes unsafe — and slow cooling is invisible, so the cook never sees it happen.

That is why every cook-chill workflow has a cooling target. There are two ways the rule is commonly written.

The 6-hour rule (Baldwin)

Douglas Baldwin's practical guidance: after pasteurizing, the center of the food must reach 130°F (54.4°C) within 6 hours, then continue down toward refrigeration temperature. That window keeps any C. perfringens growth during cooling below a factor of 10 — the accepted safety limit. Baldwin notes this is easily achieved for most sous vide items under 2 inches thick in a proper ice bath.

FDA two-stage cooling

The FDA Food Code expresses the same idea as a two-stage check you can time against the clock:

StageFrom → toWithin
Stage 1135°F → 70°F2 hours
Stage 270°F → 41°F4 more hours

If the food clears 135°F to 70°F in the first 2 hours, you have the remaining 4 hours to reach 41°F — 6 hours total. Miss the first stage and the whole batch is suspect; the early part of the cooling curve is where spores grow fastest.

Build a proper ice bath

A warm sealed bag dropped straight into the fridge cools far too slowly — the fridge has to fight the whole mass of warm food at once, and the center lags for hours. An ice-water bath pulls heat out many times faster. Baldwin's recipe for one that actually works:

  • At least as much ice as food — roughly 1 lb of ice per 1 lb of product (half ice by volume or more).
  • Top off with cold tap water so the ice surrounds the bag; water carries heat away far better than ice alone.
  • Keep the bag sealed and submerged until cold all the way through, then move it to the fridge.
  • Thinner is faster. Items over ~2 inches thick cool slowly at the center — split a thick batch into thinner portions before chilling.

How long chilling takes, by thickness

Thickness drives everything. Baldwin's cooling table gives the time for the center to reach 41°F (5°C) in an ice-water bath with at least half ice, cooling from the 130–175°F range. These are the slab-like (flat sealed bag) figures — the slowest and most realistic shape for bagged food; a cylinder or sphere of the same thickness cools noticeably faster.

ThicknessIce-bath time to 41°F (flat bag)
5 mm (0.2 in)~5 min
20 mm (0.8 in)~35 min
50 mm (2 in)~2 hr 45 min

A 2-inch slab already needs most of a working ice bath to make the 6-hour window, which is exactly why Baldwin flags 2 inches as the practical ceiling. Anything thicker should be split into thinner portions before chilling — a 100 mm (4 in) piece can take several hours at the center even in ice water, and that is where slow-cooling risk lives.

How long chilled sous vide food keeps in the fridge

Once it is cold through, the bag goes into the fridge and a second clock starts. Chilling stops fast growth, but it does not sterilize — non-proteolytic C. botulinum can still grow slowly at refrigerator temperatures, so the safe storage window depends directly on how cold your fridge actually runs. Baldwin's limits for pasteurized-then-chilled food:

Fridge temperatureSafe storage limit
Below 36.5°F (2.5°C)up to 90 days
Below 38°F (3.3°C)under 31 days
Below 41°F (5°C)under 10 days
Below 44.5°F (7°C)under 5 days

Most home refrigerators sit around 40°F, so unless you have measured a colder shelf with a thermometer, treat about 10 days as the outer limit and label every bag with the date it went in. Colder buys more time, but only if you can prove the temperature. The full breakdown is in how long sous vide food is safe in the fridge.

Reheating without undoing the work

When it is time to eat, reheat in a water bath rather than letting the food drift up slowly through the danger zone. Baldwin reheats already-pasteurized meat in a 131°F (55°C) bath — above the growth range, and right at the serving temperature for most sous vide meat. The one rule that matters: do not partially reheat and then re-chill. Every slow pass back through the warm zone is another chance for surviving spores to grow, and those passes add up.

FAQ

How fast do you have to chill sous vide food?
The center should reach 130°F (54.4°C) within 6 hours, then keep dropping to fridge temperature (Baldwin). The FDA writes it as two stages — 135°F→70°F in 2 hours, then 70°F→41°F in 4 more — 6 hours total. Clearing that first stage on time is what matters most, because spores grow fastest early in the curve.

How long does it take to chill in an ice bath?
In a bath with at least half ice, a flat sealed bag reaches 41°F in about 5 min at 5 mm, ~35 min at 20 mm (0.8 in), and ~2 hr 45 min at 50 mm (2 in) (Baldwin). Rounder shapes cool faster; over ~2 inches, split into thinner portions.

How long does chilled sous vide food keep in the fridge?
It depends on temperature: below 36.5°F up to 90 days, below 38°F under 31 days, below 41°F under 10 days, below 44.5°F under 5 days (Baldwin). Most home fridges run near 40°F, so treat ~10 days as the limit and date the bag.

How do you reheat it safely?
Reheat in a water bath — Baldwin uses 131°F (55°C) for already-pasteurized meat, above the growth range and at serving temperature. Never partially reheat and re-chill.
