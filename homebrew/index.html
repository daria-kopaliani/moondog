The two decisions every new brewer faces

Getting into home brewing really comes down to two choices, and both have boring, reliable answers. The first is extract vs all-grain: extract brewing skips the mash by using concentrated malt, cuts a brew day roughly in half, and needs less gear — which is why nearly everyone should start there and move to all-grain only when the process itself becomes the hobby. The extract vs all-grain guide lays out the honest trade-offs without the forum snobbery.

The second is gear. A first five-gallon batch needs a kettle, a fermenter with an airlock, a siphon, a hydrometer, bottles — and sanitizer, the one purchase that separates drinkable beer from drain-pour. The equipment guide ranks a beginner setup piece by piece so you don't buy the shiny stuff first.

Bottling day is arithmetic, not luck

Flat beer and exploding bottles have the same cause: guessed priming sugar. The carbonation math is simple — roughly 4 grams of corn sugar per liter of beer per volume of CO₂ you want to add. A five-gallon batch carbonated to a typical 2.5 volumes works out to about 121 g (4.3 oz) of corn sugar, dissolved and mixed in at bottling. The priming sugar guide has the worked example and a reference table by style, batch size, and target fizz.

Common questions

How long until I'm drinking it? For a standard ale: about two weeks in the fermenter, then two to three weeks conditioning in the bottle. Rushing either stage is the classic first-batch mistake — green beer just needs calendar time, not intervention.

Is brewing at home actually cheaper? Per pint, yes, once the starter gear is paid off over a few batches. But the honest pitch isn't savings — it's control. You pick the style, the bitterness, the carbonation level, and bottling day teaches you more about beer than a decade of drinking it.
