How to can tomato juice (acidified): time by the book

Updated 20264 min read

Tomato juice (acidified) is high-acid, so a boiling-water bath is safe — here are the USDA processing times and how time changes with elevation.

How long do you can tomato juice (acidified)?

USDA processes tomato juice (acidified) for 35 minutes for pints and 40 minutes for quarts, at a full rolling boil, jars covered by at least an inch of water. Add 2 Tbsp bottled lemon juice per quart (1 Tbsp per pint).

The acid, exactly

Every jar gets acid before filling — modern tomato varieties can sit right at the pH 4.6 safety line, so USDA doesn't leave it to the tomato:

  • Bottled lemon juice: 1 tablespoon per pint, 2 tablespoons per quart. Bottled, because its acidity is standardized; fresh lemons vary too much.
  • Citric acid: ¼ teaspoon per pint, ½ teaspoon per quart.
  • Vinegar (5% acidity): 2 tablespoons per pint, 4 tablespoons per quart — safe per NCHFP, but it can shift the flavor, so most people stick with lemon juice or citric acid.

Salt is a different story: 1 teaspoon per quart is optional, flavor only. Sugar may be added to soften the acid taste — the acid itself never comes out. Fill hot juice into jars leaving ½-inch headspace.

How many tomatoes you need

Juice goes further than sauce: figure an average of 3¼ pounds of tomatoes per quart. A 53-pound bushel yields 15–18 quarts of juice, per NCHFP. To make the juice, simmer cut tomatoes until soft, then press through a sieve or food mill to remove skins and seeds — then acidify, reheat to boiling, and fill.

Altitude adjustment

The 35/40-minute times hold from 0–1,000 ft. Above that, water-bath times step up: pints 40 min at 1,001–3,000 ft, 45 min at 3,001–6,000 ft, 50 min above 6,000 ft; quarts run 5 minutes longer at each band (45/50/55). Boiling water gets cooler with elevation, so jars need longer at temperature.

The pressure-canner alternative

USDA also publishes tested pressure options for tomato juice: with a dial-gauge canner, 20 minutes at 6 lb (0–2,000 ft); with a weighted-gauge canner, 20 minutes at 5 lb (0–1,000 ft, one step up above that). The jars still get the same added acid either way. Choosing between methods? See water bath vs. pressure canning.

Why tomato juice (acidified) is water-bath canned

Tomato juice (acidified) is high-acid (pH 4.6 or below), once the recipe's added acid is included, so a boiling-water bath reaches a safe temperature. Skipping the acidification step is the classic deadly mistake — do not omit it. Leave the headspace your tested recipe lists and check every lid sealed before storing.

FAQ

How long do you can tomato juice (acidified)?

USDA processes tomato juice (acidified) for 35 minutes for pints and 40 minutes for quarts, at a full boil, adjusting time for altitude.

Do you water bath or pressure can tomato juice (acidified)?

Tomato juice (acidified) is high-acid, so a boiling-water bath is safe as long as the recipe's added acid is included.

How do you adjust tomato juice (acidified) for altitude?

At altitude a boiling-water bath needs more time: add 5 minutes at 1,001–3,000 ft, 10 minutes at 3,001–6,000 ft, 15 minutes at 6,001–8,000 ft and 20 minutes above 8,000 ft.

How many tomatoes for a quart of juice?

About 3¼ pounds per quart — a 53-lb bushel yields 15–18 quarts, per NCHFP.

Do you add salt to canned tomato juice?

Optional: 1 teaspoon per quart for flavor only. It plays no safety role — the required addition is the acid (2 Tbsp bottled lemon juice or ½ tsp citric acid per quart).

Can you pressure can tomato juice instead?

Yes — USDA lists 20 minutes at 6 lb (dial-gauge, 0–2,000 ft) or 5 lb (weighted-gauge, 0–1,000 ft). The same acidification is still required.
