Water bath vs. pressure canning: which one your food needs

The dividing line is acidity — and getting it wrong is a botulism risk.

This is a food-safety topic.

Low-acid foods canned incorrectly can grow Clostridium botulinum, which causes botulism. The guidance below summarizes USDA / National Center for Home Food Preservation (NCHFP) positions. Always follow a current USDA-tested recipe for your specific food — never improvise canning times or methods.

There are two home canning methods, and which one you must use is not a preference — it's determined by the acidity of the food.

What's the difference between water bath and pressure canning?

Water-bath canning heats jars in boiling water — 212°F at sea level — and is safe only for high-acid foods (pH 4.6 or below). Pressure canning reaches about 240°F under pressure and is required for low-acid foods (above pH 4.6), because boiling water alone can't destroy Clostridium botulinum spores. The two methods are not interchangeable: which one you must use is decided by the food's acidity, not by convenience.

The pH 4.6 dividing line

Per USDA/NCHFP, the controlling factor is whether a food is high-acid or low-acid, split at pH 4.6:

Food typepHMethod (per USDA)
High-acid4.6 or belowBoiling-water bath (or atmospheric steam) is sufficient
Low-acidabove 4.6Pressure canning is required

Boiling water (212°F at sea level) can't get hot enough to destroy botulinum spores. Only a pressure canner reaches the ~240°F that USDA-tested processes require for low-acid foods.

Two USDA cautions worth repeating:

Tomatoes sit near the line — USDA recipes call for added acid (bottled lemon juice or citric acid) for safe water-bath processing, and the same rule governs home-canned salsa. And pumpkin/winter-squash purée or mashed has no USDA-tested process — USDA approves only cubed pumpkin, pressure-canned. When in doubt, follow the tested recipe exactly.

FAQ

What is the difference between water bath and pressure canning?
A water bath heats jars in boiling water (212°F at sea level) and is safe only for high-acid foods (pH 4.6 or below). Pressure canning reaches ~240°F and is required for low-acid foods (above 4.6), because boiling water can't kill botulinum spores. The food's acidity decides the method — not preference. (USDA/NCHFP.)

Should I use water bath or pressure canning?
Water bath for high-acid foods — most fruits, jams, pickles, properly acidified tomatoes and salsa. Pressure canner for low-acid foods — all plain vegetables, meats, poultry, seafood, soups. At or below pH 4.6 → water bath; above 4.6 → pressure.

Can I pressure can high-acid foods too?
Yes — a pressure canner can process high-acid foods safely, it just isn't required. The reverse is never allowed: you can't substitute a boiling-water bath for a food that needs pressure.

Why do tomatoes need added acid?
Many tomato varieties sit right at pH 4.6, so USDA requires added bottled lemon juice or citric acid to guarantee they're acidic enough for a water bath. (NCHFP.)
