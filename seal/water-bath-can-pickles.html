How long to water bath can pickles

Updated 20264 min read

Pickles are high-acid, so a boiling-water bath is the correct method — no pressure canner needed. The processing time depends on your recipe and jar size, and the wait before eating is a separate question. Here are the USDA numbers for both.

How long by jar size

The time comes from your tested recipe, not the jar alone — but the two most-canned cucumber pickles set the pattern. At 0–1,000 ft, at a full rolling boil with jars covered by at least an inch of water:

  • Quick fresh-pack dill pickles — 10 minutes for pints, 15 minutes for quarts.
  • Bread-and-butter pickles — 10 minutes for both pints and quarts.

Quarts often need longer than pints because a bigger jar takes longer to heat through, but not always — bread-and-butter runs the same time for both. That is exactly why you follow the recipe's own table rather than guessing from jar size. For half-pint jars, use the pint time: never process for less than the smallest jar size your recipe lists. Leave the ½-inch headspace the recipe calls for.

How long after canning before you can eat them

This is the other half of the question, and it has a different answer. The pickles are safe to eat as soon as the jars seal and cool — you confirm the seal after 12 to 24 hours and then they are shelf-stable. But flavor keeps developing for weeks as the brine works into the cucumber. NCHFP's own bread-and-butter recipe advises storing jars 4 to 5 weeks to develop ideal flavor, and traditional fermented dills cure for about 3 weeks before they taste right. So: safe in a day, best in a month.

Altitude adjustment

A boiling-water bath needs more time as elevation rises, because water boils cooler. Starting from the sea-level time, add: 5 minutes at 1,001–3,000 ft, 10 minutes at 3,001–6,000 ft, 15 minutes at 6,001–8,000 ft, and 20 minutes above 8,000 ft. The altitude calculator does this for you.

Why water bath is safe for pickles

Pickles are high-acid (pH 4.6 or below) because of the vinegar brine, so a boiling-water bath reaches a safe temperature — a pressure canner is not required. Use commercial 5% vinegar and do not reduce the vinegar or dilute the brine below what the tested recipe specifies; the acid is what makes water-bath canning safe here. If you want crisper pickles, USDA allows a low-temperature pasteurization alternative — hold the water at 180–185°F for 30 minutes, checked with a thermometer so it never drops below 180°F. It is not a shortcut; it is a different tested process, and going below 180°F is unsafe.

FAQ

How long do you water bath can pickles?

It depends on the recipe and jar size. USDA processes quick fresh-pack dill 10 minutes for pints and 15 minutes for quarts, and bread-and-butter 10 minutes for both, at a full boil at 0–1,000 ft, then add time for altitude. Always use your tested recipe's time.

Pint vs quart jars of pickles?

Quarts usually need longer than pints. Fresh-pack dill is 10 min pints / 15 min quarts; bread-and-butter is 10 min for both. For half-pints, use the pint time — never less than the smallest jar size in the recipe.

How long after canning can you eat them?

Safe once the jars seal and cool (checked after 12–24 hours), but flavor develops for weeks. NCHFP's bread-and-butter recipe suggests 4–5 weeks for ideal flavor; fermented dills cure about 3 weeks.

Do you water bath or pressure can pickles?

Water bath. The vinegar brine makes pickles high-acid, so a boiling-water bath is safe — use 5% vinegar and don't cut it.
